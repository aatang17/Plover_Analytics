# AeroEdge (7409): contracted LEAP turbine-blade supply, and the reinvestment behind its first diversification

*Initiation of coverage · Asia Economics Observations · 27 September 2026 · Data as of 27 September 2026 · No rating*

## Summary

AeroEdge Co., Ltd. (TSE Growth: 7409) is one of two companies worldwide that machine the titanium-aluminide low-pressure-turbine blades fitted to the CFM LEAP engine, which powers the Airbus A320neo family and the Boeing 737 MAX. It sells almost all of its output to Safran Aircraft Engines under a contract that runs to 2034 and allocates it a share of Safran's blade requirement: 40% since July 2024, rising to the high-40s from January 2028. Safran accounted for 96.8% of sales in the fiscal year ended June 2025 and, on the company's own figure, 97.1% in the year ended June 2026.

Sales rose 41.2% to ¥5,084m in FY6/26 and operating income 74.8% to ¥1,145m, an operating margin of 22.5%. The company guides to sales of ¥7,950m (+56.4%) and operating income of ¥1,550m (+35.3%) for FY6/27. The guidance marks the first material change in the revenue mix since listing: non-blade sales, from a second engine programme and a newly acquired airframe-parts subsidiary, are guided to 29.9% of the total, against 9.1% in FY6/26.

Three findings are material. First, blade volume has tracked between 39% and 43% of LEAP engine deliveries in each of the last four July–June years, which is consistent with the contracted share and indicates that revenue is driven by LEAP output rather than by order wins. Second, operating margin has not risen with volume. The contribution margin on blade processing is high because Safran supplies the material free of charge, but management is reinvesting it in its own blade material, new programmes and headcount, and targets a margin of about 20% while doing so. Third, the investment programme is funded by operating cash, government subsidies and bank debt rather than new equity. Subsidies of ¥1,979m were the largest single source of cash in FY6/26.

The principal risk is contractual. Safran guarantees neither a minimum volume nor its forecasts, firm orders cover only a few weeks, and renewal is not automatic. Foreign investors held 2.6% of the register at June 2025, and five strategic and founding holders own 56.1% of the shares. This note carries no rating or valuation; the platform does not hold share prices.

## Product, customer and contract terms

AeroEdge was established on 16 September 2015 as a spin-out of Kikuchi Gear, a gear maker in Ashikaga, Tochigi prefecture, to execute a blade supply agreement that Kikuchi Gear had signed with Safran in 2013. The company describes this as the first direct supply contract between Safran and a Japanese small or medium-sized manufacturer. First deliveries followed in 2016, and the company listed on the TSE Growth market in 2023.

The product is the titanium-aluminide (TiAl) blade in the rear stages of the LEAP low-pressure turbine. TiAl weighs about half as much as the nickel alloys it replaces and retains strength at high temperature, but it is hard and brittle, which makes it difficult to machine at production volumes. The company states that only two suppliers deliver LEAP TiAl blades in series; it does not name the other, described in its disclosures as "overseas company A", and this note does not identify it. LEAP is the sole engine on the 737 MAX and one of two engine options on the A320neo family. According to Japan Aircraft Development Corporation data reproduced by the company, the two aircraft families had a combined backlog of 12,873 aircraft at the end of June 2026, 77% of Airbus's and Boeing's total commercial backlog.

The commercial terms are set out in the risk section of the annual securities report and are summarised below.

The structure concentrates risk in one counterparty but limits it in two respects. The share allocation fixes AeroEdge's position against the other supplier for the life of the contract, and the free-issue material arrangement removes raw-material price risk, which the company illustrates with the volatility of rutile, the ore from which titanium sponge is produced. The same arrangement also means that AeroEdge's revenue depends on a single upstream material supplier, whose delivery delays have constrained output in the past.

## Blade volume against LEAP engine deliveries

The company's main operating indicator converts blade shipments into engine-set equivalents: blades sold divided by the number of blades per LEAP engine, on the assumption that every blade goes into a new engine. Set against CFM's LEAP deliveries over the same July–June periods, the ratio has stayed between 38.7% and 43.4% for four years.

The annual growth rates diverge by no more than about ten percentage points. In FY6/25, volume rose 11.5% while LEAP deliveries rose 1.6%, which is consistent with the step-up in contracted share from 35% to 40% in July 2024. In FY6/26 volume rose 32.6% against LEAP delivery growth of 42.9%. Safran reported LEAP deliveries of 1,030 units in the first half of 2026, an increase of 41%, and raised its full-year 2026 guidance to growth in the "high-teens" from about 15%. The company expects engine-set equivalents of 1,084 in FY6/27 (+28.0%), which includes part of the share increase due in January 2028.

The backlog supports a long production run. The company reports backlogs of 7,469 A320neo-family and 5,404 737 MAX aircraft at the end of June 2026, each equivalent to 12.3 years of 2025 deliveries (607 and 440 aircraft respectively). Two constraints on near-term rates are noted in the company's own materials: A320neo output has been held back by delivery delays of the competing Pratt & Whitney PW1100G engine, and Boeing's 737 MAX rate remains subject to FAA approval, which lifted a 38-per-month cap to 42 per month with a planned move to 47 per month around mid-2026.

## Sales, operating income and margin, FY6/19 to FY6/27E

Sales fell from ¥2,595m in FY6/19 to ¥848m in FY6/21 as aircraft production collapsed during the pandemic, and have since risen six-fold to ¥5,084m. The company made operating losses from its founding until FY6/22 and has been profitable since FY6/23.

![Sales have recovered six-fold since FY6/21; operating margin has held near 20%](charts/02-sales-and-margin.png)

Operating margin has moved within a band of 16.4% to 22.5% since the return to profit, rather than rising steadily with volume. Two years illustrate why.

In FY6/25, sales rose 7.5% but operating income fell 7.1% to ¥655m, and the margin fell from 21.1% to 18.2%. Gross margin improved from 45.1% to 46.8% in the same year, so the decline is attributable to selling, general and administrative expenses, which rose 27.8% to ¥1,031m, including research and development of ¥200m against ¥140m a year earlier.

In FY6/26, the margin of 22.5% was flattered by a one-off item. The company booked about ¥300m of contract-development sales relating to the new blade material in the second quarter, which it describes as high-margin. Excluding that amount, sales growth would have been about 33% rather than 41.2%. The company's operating-income bridge attributes ¥1,386m of the increase to higher sales, and ¥96m to foreign exchange, offset mainly by variable costs (¥329m), personnel (¥279m), maintenance and consumables (¥95m), costs related to the Ono Plant acquisition (¥55m) and inventory and other items (¥158m).

For FY6/27 the company guides to a margin of 19.5%. It attributes the decline to development spending on its own blade material, a rise in depreciation from ¥417m to ¥767m, continued hiring, cost inflation and the consolidation of the lower-margin Ono Plant. Management's stated policy is to invest while holding the operating margin at about 20%. On that policy, incremental contribution from blade volume is being reinvested rather than reported as margin expansion, and the margin trajectory is a function of the investment programme as much as of LEAP output.

## Unit economics and foreign-exchange exposure

Because Safran supplies the material, AeroEdge's revenue is a processing fee, and its cost base is dominated by labour, tooling, depreciation and utilities rather than materials. The company cites a material-cost ratio of 0% for its blade business, against 41% for Japanese small and medium-sized manufacturers on the Small and Medium Enterprise Agency's survey. The low variable-cost structure is what gives blade volume its high contribution margin.

Sales are invoiced in US dollars, while costs are almost entirely in yen. The company estimates that each ¥1 move in USD/JPY changes sales and operating income by ¥40–50m, with a weaker yen positive; its FY6/27 guidance assumes ¥152 per dollar. The average rate applied, including forward contracts, was ¥145 in FY6/24, ¥148 in FY6/25 and ¥152 in FY6/26. Since the October 2024 contract amendment, part of the selling price adjusts when the exchange rate moves outside an agreed band, which reduces the exposure to large moves but leaves the company exposed within the band.

## Growth programmes and the FY6/27 revenue mix

The company organises its growth plan in four stages: higher blade volume and share, its own blade material, new parts programmes, and eventually engine maintenance, repair and overhaul (MRO). The FY6/27 guidance is the first year in which the third stage is material to reported sales.

![Non-blade revenue is guided to reach 30% of sales in FY6/27](charts/03-sales-by-source.png)

*Blade processing.* Guided blade-processing sales of ¥5,574m in FY6/27 are 20.5% higher than in FY6/26. Around ¥2.4bn of capacity investment is in progress to meet higher LEAP rates and the January 2028 share increase.

*Own blade material.* AeroEdge has developed its own cast TiAl blade material to replace the material that Safran currently sources from a single European supplier and issues free of charge. Safran has contracted AeroEdge to supply part of the material from FY6/27 and all of it for AeroEdge's share from January 2028, under a supply contract running from July 2026 to 2034. The company describes an integrated material-and-machining supply as a first in the industry. Investment is about ¥2.6bn, of which roughly half is expected to be covered by subsidy, with a production plant due in early 2027. The company expects costs to lead revenue in FY6/27 and profit contribution from FY6/29. The annual report notes that casting is a process in which the company has no prior production experience.

*New programmes.* The Engine B programme, a part for a non-LEAP aircraft engine for an undisclosed global aerospace manufacturer, entered series production in FY6/26 under a contract running from 2026 to 2036, with guided sales of about ¥796m in FY6/27. The Engine A programme, also for a non-LEAP engine, has been delayed by about a year because the customer required additional engine tests; the company now expects series production from June–August 2027 and a profit contribution from FY6/28. Investment in the two programmes, about ¥1.9bn and ¥1.7bn, is complete, and subsidies are expected to cover roughly two-thirds and one-half of it respectively.

*Ono Plant.* AeroEdge acquired all of Ono Plant, an aluminium airframe-parts machinist in Kaminokawa, Tochigi, with 58 employees and domestic heavy-industry customers across civil and defence work, for about ¥0.98bn. Only the balance sheet was consolidated in FY6/26; its guided FY6/27 sales are ¥1,192m. The company expects margin improvement at Ono Plant to take two to three years.

*MRO.* The company is researching additive-manufacturing repair techniques with customers and has no large investment planned for FY6/27.

Taken together, blade processing is guided to fall to 70.1% of sales in FY6/27 from about 91% in FY6/26. Engine B and Ono Plant are the main sources of the change. Customer concentration will fall correspondingly, although Safran will remain the dominant counterparty.

## Cash flow, subsidies and funding

The investment programme has been funded by operating cash, government subsidies and bank borrowing. Subsidies are the swing factor. In FY6/26, operating cash flow of ¥2,959m included ¥1,979m of subsidies received; excluding them, operating cash flow was ¥980m on a simple deduction, or ¥1.01bn on the company's own basis. Spending on fixed assets, mainly for the new programmes, was ¥2,508m, and the Ono Plant acquisition used a further ¥709m.

![Subsidies exceeded underlying operating cash flow in FY6/26](charts/04-cash-flow.png)

The company raised ¥1,900m of new long-term loans and repaid ¥535m in FY6/26, and has signed new loan agreements totalling ¥3.8bn, including a term loan with a commitment line. Year-end cash was ¥2,628m and the net debt-to-equity ratio 0.6x, against a stated ceiling of about 1.5x. At the end of FY6/25, borrowings of ¥3,350m represented 40.8% of total assets, and some loan agreements carry financial covenants. The equity ratio was 39.1% at the end of FY6/26.

Management ranks its funding sources as operating cash first, borrowing second, and equity only for a large investment opportunity. The record is consistent with that ranking. There has been no share placement since the 2023 listing. The number of shares outstanding rose 7.5% in FY6/26 on a split-adjusted basis, from 23,060,274 to 24,780,972, in several steps visible in the large-shareholding reports, while capital stock rose only from ¥495.6m to ¥568m. That pattern is consistent with the exercise of stock options rather than an offering. The FY6/26 annual report, due by 30 September 2026, will confirm the source. Outstanding options represented 578,000 potential shares at the end of FY6/25, equivalent to 15.0% of issued shares, and diluted earnings per share of ¥29.63 in FY6/26 were 9.6% below basic earnings per share of ¥32.77. The company has not paid a dividend and does not plan to for the time being.

## Ownership and governance

AeroEdge is controlled in practice by a group of strategic and founding shareholders. At 30 June 2026 the five largest holders were Kikuchi Gear (17.4%), Toyota Tsusho (11.1%), chief executive Jun Morinishi (11.1%), DMG Mori (9.7%) and the Development Bank of Japan (6.8%), together 56.1% of shares outstanding.

![Five strategic and founding holders own 56% of the shares](charts/05-holders.png)

Foreign investors held 2.6% of the register at June 2025, individuals 27.6% and other corporations 43.9%. The Development Bank of Japan reduced its holding from 11.61% to 7.07% in a report filed in December 2025, and the chief executive's holding under the statutory large-shareholding measure fell from 15.58% to 13.63% in a report filed in May 2026; that measure includes potential shares and is not comparable with the register percentage. The only short position disclosed under the JPX 0.5% threshold is Goldman Sachs International at 0.6% (calculation date 11 September 2026).

The board has seven members including the audit-and-supervisory members, one of whom is a woman. The annual report identifies dependence on the chief executive, the founder, who led the original Safran negotiation while at Kikuchi Gear, as a risk. The company split its shares 3-for-1 on 1 January 2026 and 2-for-1 on 1 July 2026 to improve liquidity.

## Risks

*Contract and counterparty.* The absence of a minimum volume, the short firm-order horizon and the non-automatic renewal mean that the contracted share does not translate into contracted revenue. A LEAP production stoppage would allow Safran to suspend AeroEdge's line without compensation.

*Aircraft production rates.* Revenue is tied to A320neo-family and 737 MAX output. The annual report notes the FAA's 2024 restriction on 737 MAX production increases; the company's materials note that A320neo output has been constrained by the competing PW1100G engine.

*Material supply and the new-material ramp.* Blade material currently comes from a limited number of sources, and past supply delays have reduced output. The switch to AeroEdge's own cast material removes that dependence but introduces execution risk in a process that is new to the company; delay or low yield would affect blade sales as a whole.

*New programmes.* Engine A has already slipped by about a year. The company notes that new programmes typically take about two fiscal years from award to profitability.

*Foreign exchange.* Operating income moves by ¥40–50m for each ¥1 change in USD/JPY within the contractual band.

*Concentration of assets.* Production is at a single plant in Tochigi prefecture, plus Ono Plant nearby.

*Dilution.* Outstanding options equal to 15.0% of issued shares at FY6/25.
